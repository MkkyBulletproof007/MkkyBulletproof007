# Negotiation SOP (Chris Voss Playbook, adapted)

## Read This First: Don't Overuse These
These are for real friction, not everyday communication. Most client messages
(scheduling, logistics, delivery updates, routine check-ins) need **zero**
tactics from this file — just plain, direct communication per
`context/brand_voice.md`.

Hard rules:
- **Never stack more than one tactic in a single message.** Two "psychological"
  phrases in one email reads as scripted and manipulative — that's exactly
  what the "cringe" kill word in `brand_voice.md` is guarding against.
- Reach for these only when there's an actual problem to solve: a deal went
  cold, a client is lowballing, an ask is unreasonable, or a conversation is
  stuck. Don't use them to "optimize" a normal, friendly exchange.
- Adapt the wording to Mykyta's actual voice — a bit more formal than casual
  chat, direct, no fluff, no emojis. Don't paste Voss's exact hostage-negotiation
  phrasing if it sounds clinical or out of place for a filmmaker talking to a
  small business owner. Match natural speech.
- Anything sent to a client follows the punctuation rule in `brand_voice.md`:
  no em dashes, double hyphens, or parentheses.
- If in doubt, don't use a tactic — send the plain, honest version instead.

## The Toolkit

### 1. Reactivation — the last resort for a deal that has gone quiet
Use this only after follow-ups with new angles have gone unanswered (see the
follow-up rules in `context/memory.md`). It is the last message, not the
first. Send, close to word-for-word: **"Have you given up on [the project]?"**
Don't soften it or add a paragraph of context around it — the effect depends
on its bluntness. Then read the response:
- **Fast agreement / re-engagement** → they're back in, move forward.
- **Dead silence** → that's your answer. They've moved on. Stop following up.
- **"No, not at all, just..."** → they're close but something's in the way —
  follow up asking directly what's stopping things from moving forward.

Check the client's Notion row or `clients/[name]/profile.md` first — e.g.
Andreas Tsimon is already flagged stalled; don't re-send this without a real
reason to believe something's changed.

### 2. Forced empathy — an unreasonable ask
When a client asks for something unreasonable (half the price, free extra
deliverables, an impossible turnaround), respond with: **"How am I supposed
to do that?"** — not defensively, genuinely curious. It makes them look at
your side of the table without you having to argue your position. Then stop
talking and let them respond.

### 3. No-oriented openers — reaching out cold or after friction
Instead of a yes-seeking opener, offer a no. "Do you have a few minutes to
talk about the project?" becomes **"Is now a bad time to talk about the
project?"** Both answers you get back are useful — either they say no it's
fine (and give you full attention), or they tell you exactly when a better
time is.

### 4. Accusations audit — before a hesitant pitch or hard proposal
Before pitching a nervous or price-sensitive prospect, name the objections
they're probably thinking before they say them: e.g. *"You're probably
thinking I'm a one-person operation, or that this is more than you planned to
spend on video."* Naming it first takes the power out of it — don't soften
the biggest objection, lead with it.

### 5. "That's right" vs. "you're right" — confirming scope/briefs
When you summarize a client's brief or requirements back to them before
starting work, listen for which phrase comes back:
- **"That's right"** → they feel fully heard, your understanding is correct,
  proceed.
- **"You're right" / "yeah" / "sure"** → weak, surface-level agreement — there's
  likely something they haven't said yet. Summarize again, more thoroughly,
  before starting work based on it.

### 6. The generous rejection — countering a lowball offer
When a client's offer is too low, use the two-part phrase: **"That's
generous. It just doesn't work for me."** Don't follow it with a
counter-number immediately; let it sit. This pairs directly with
`.claude/skills/pricing-guard/SKILL.md` — it's the phrasing that avoids
justifying your rate against "market rate" or a competitor's price (external
criteria you'll never win an argument over).

### 7. "What makes you ask?" — a client question with an unclear motive
When a client asks something and you can't tell why (e.g. "are you available
long-term?", "do you have insurance?", "have you worked with bigger brands?"),
don't just answer at face value — ask **"What makes you ask?"** first. Never
"why" — it reads as accusatory. This surfaces the real concern so you answer
the actual question, not the surface one.

### 8. Labels for tired/rushed conversations
When a client seems rushed, tired, or a conversation is dragging, swap a
direct question for an observation they just confirm or correct: **"It seems
like the budget is the sticking point here"** instead of "what's the
problem?" Lower effort for them to respond to, especially later in the day.
